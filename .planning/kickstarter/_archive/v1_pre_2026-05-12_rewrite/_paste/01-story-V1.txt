A murder mystery in your kitchen. Five phones. One bartender's manual from 1862.

Speakeater is a multiplayer party game built on the cocktail-history record from 1862 to 1923.

Eight guests at the table. Each one a phone in their hand. Each phone running Speakeater, joined to the same game via a four-letter code. Each guest a character. Each guest a secret. The murder, the cast, the cocktails, and the food are all pulled from primary sources you can verify on Internet Archive.

The drinks are real. The food is real. The mystery is the part you play.

I built this so a person at home tonight can host an evening that does what a $200-a-head murder mystery night does, with primary-source cocktail history layered underneath it, for the price of a single bottle.

What's already built

The Speakeater app is real. Native Android. On Google Play June 10, 2026.

I photographed every American bartender's manuscript from 1862 to 1923. I transcribed 2,846 cocktails by hand from those manuscripts. Original text preserved alongside the modernized recipe. Original typography preserved, including the long-s that reads as "f" in the 1916 Ensslin. That mode is called Bootlegger.

Beyond those: 2,848 pre-Prohibition cocktails sourced from the period. 6,539 drinks total counting modern revival classics and the 1,361-strong temperance mocktail set. 23,743 food recipes ranked by what you already own when you photograph the fridge.

The first five Mystery Nights are written and ready to ship as interactive multiplayer games on day one. Internal Testing build the week this campaign closes, at $19 or above.

How a Mystery Night works

You and seven guests are at the dinner table. The food is in front of you. The cocktails are mixed.

You tap "Host a Game" in the Speakeater app. A four-letter code appears on your phone. RYAS. Or LION. Or TANG. The other seven guests open Speakeater, tap "Join a Game," and enter the code. Their phones light up with their character. Name. Role. Secret only they know.

You tap Begin.

For the next three hours, the game runs across all eight phones at once. Some beats are broadcast. Everyone sees them. Some beats are private. Only one player's phone lights up with a twist, a clue, a multiple-choice action. What that player chooses changes what other players see five minutes later. Each player drives part of the story. The reveal lands at the last cocktail.

The five Mystery Nights ready at launch:

Murder at the Algonquin. Manhattan, 1924. The Round Table set. The theatre critic is dead.
The Bootlegger's Wife. Chicago, 1929. A basement bar after a raid. Someone tipped the feds.
Last Toast at the Ritz. Paris, 1925. Frank Meier's bar at closing. The wealthy patron just collapsed.
Heir to the Pendennis. Louisville, 1923. Derby Eve. The old colonel's will reading is tomorrow. He won't see it.
The Vanishing Socialite. West Egg, 1926. A young woman left the ballroom at midnight. She never made it home.

Each game ships with five characters, a hook, multiple-choice actions, plot twists, and the reveal.

And the food and drinks are real

Every Mystery Night ships with the full Curate-a-Party menu underneath it. Five drinks. Six small plates. A host timeline from the day before through zero hour. A shopping list grouped by aisle. A music note. The Algonquin night pours the Aviation, the Sidecar, the Manhattan, the Old Fashioned, the Champagne Cocktail. Period-correct.

Beyond the five Mystery Nights, fifty additional Curate-a-Party menus ship across the next twelve months. Garden parties. Hotel-bar nights. Holiday menus. Hand-written from the primary sources, same as the cocktails.

This is the part nobody else is doing. Jackbox doesn't pour drinks. Hunt-A-Killer doesn't write recipes. Speakeater is the only place where the game on your phone and the bottle on your table are the same product.

# A Gatsby Affair

**Source:** F. Scott Fitzgerald, *The Great Gatsby*, 1925, party scenes chapters 3 and 6. Albert Stevens Crockett, *Old Waldorf Bar Days*, 1931. Long Island estate hire-bar inventories of the period.
**Setting:** West Egg, Long Island, 1925. Black tie. The party that lasts until sunrise. The host has been seen exactly once.
**Guests:** 16 to 20. Five hours. 8pm onward.

---

## The drinks

**Gin Rickey.** Joe Rickey's invention, Shoomaker's Bar, Washington D.C., 1880s. The bourbon version came first. Gin took over by the 1900s. The drink Gatsby himself orders, in chapter seven: "we drank gin rickeys with the boys." The 1925 reader knew exactly what was in his hand.

Two ounces gin. Half ounce lime. Soda water. Lime wedge. Build in a highball over ice.

**Mint Julep.** Kentucky bourbon tradition since the 1830s. Era-appropriate for the South-fascinated wealthy of West Egg, who collected Confederate cavalry sabers and quoted Walt Whitman without reading him.

Twelve mint leaves. Half ounce simple syrup. Two and a half ounces bourbon. Muddle the mint with the syrup in a julep cup or rocks glass. Pack with crushed ice. Pour the bourbon over. Stir lightly. Top with more crushed ice. Mint bouquet.

**Pink Lady.** Jack Dunn's invention, attributed to a New York hotel bar around 1912. The drink that signaled what kind of party it was.

Two ounces gin. Half ounce applejack. Half ounce grenadine. Half ounce lemon. One egg white. Dry shake. Wet shake with ice. Strain into a coupe.

**Brandy Alexander.** Troy Alexander, Rector's, New York, 1922. Built for a wedding party celebrating Phoebe Snow, the fictional clean-clothes mascot of the Lackawanna Railroad. The recipe outlived the railroad.

One ounce cognac. One ounce crème de cacao. One ounce heavy cream. Shake hard with ice. Strain into a coupe. Grated nutmeg.

**Champagne, simply.** Veuve Clicquot or Pol Roger if budget allows. Iced. Open on the half-hour. The drink Gatsby's parties actually ran on.

## The food

The 1925 Long Island estate party was not a meal. It was a five-hour grazing arc, plated on long tables that ran the length of a room. Guests served themselves and drifted back, and the ice on the caviar service had to be reset twice across the night. Nothing here is dinner. Everything is bites that survive a coupe in one hand and a conversation in the other.

**Caviar service.** One ounce of American osetra or paddlefish caviar per four guests. Buckwheat blinis, warm. Crème fraîche. Minced shallot. Chopped chive. Lemon wedges. Mother-of-pearl spoons. The single most period-correct expense.

**Oysters on the half-shell.** Two per guest. Lemon. Mignonette (red wine vinegar, shallot, cracked pepper). Horseradish. Tabasco. The American oyster bar of 1925 in miniature.

**Cold poached lobster medallions.** Remoulade alongside. Half a lobster tail per guest, pre-poached, chilled, sliced into rounds.

**Beef tenderloin canapés.** Rare-roasted tenderloin, sliced thin. Toast points. Horseradish cream. Watercress. Three per guest.

**Stuffed mushrooms.** White button mushrooms stuffed with crab and breadcrumbs, baked. The hot bite that keeps people from getting too drunk too fast.

**Chocolate-dipped strawberries.** Plated at midnight. Twenty-four for sixteen guests. The dessert that ends up in someone's coat pocket on the way out.

## Host timeline

The day before:
- Order the oysters and caviar for next-day pickup.
- Pre-poach the lobster. Chill on a rack.
- Roast the beef tenderloin rare. Chill.

Four hours before guests arrive:
- Slice the lobster medallions. Make the remoulade. Slice the beef paper-thin. Cover and refrigerate everything.

Three hours before:
- Make the mignonette. Arrange for the oysters to be shucked at home, or steel yourself for an hour with a knife. Par-bake the stuffed mushrooms.

Two hours before:
- Cut and toast the toast points. Warm the blinis. Assemble the beef canapés.
- Plate the caviar service on a bed of crushed ice in a chilled silver dish.

Ninety minutes before:
- Set up two bar stations. Sixteen guests on a five-hour party needs two bars. Iced champagne in standing buckets. The bottle pours by the dozen.
- Chill every coupe.

Sixty minutes before:
- Plate the chocolate-dipped strawberries on a silver tray. Cover. Refrigerate.
- Final check. Walk every room. Stand at the front door for a moment.

Thirty minutes before:
- Pour a Gin Rickey for yourself on the front porch.
- Reheat the stuffed mushrooms in a low oven.
- Bring up the music.

Zero:
- The first guests arrive. Open the first champagne audibly. The party becomes itself.

## Music

Hot jazz, dance jazz. Paul Whiteman's orchestra. The Original Dixieland Jass Band on Victor reissues. Florence Mills sides. 100 to 130 BPM. A live pianist if the budget allows, period-appropriate sheet music available in the app extras.

Caviar (4 to 6 oz, paddlefish for value, osetra for the gesture). Buckwheat blinis (24, store-bought is fine). Crème fraîche. Shallots. Chives. Mother-of-pearl spoons (rentable). Oysters (32 to 40). Mignonette ingredients. Tabasco. Lobster tails (8). Remoulade ingredients. Beef tenderloin (1.5 lbs). Watercress. Horseradish. White button mushrooms (24). Crabmeat (8oz). Breadcrumbs. Roughly $625 for sixteen guests, excluding the spirits. This is the most expensive menu in the set. The era's parties were not cheap.

## Faithful and modern-flexible

The caviar may scale to paddlefish without anyone of taste objecting. Osetra is the gesture, paddlefish is the value, and the difference at one ounce per four guests is hard to taste. Oysters can be replaced with pickled shrimp for guests with shellfish reservations. Lobster medallions become shrimp cocktail for tighter budgets. The Brandy Alexander has a faithful non-alcoholic substitute in the temperance database, the Cocoa Cream Float, circa 1928. Vegetarian guests get mushroom pâté on toast and tomato-jam crostini in place of the lobster and beef canapés. Both are period-correct American hotel-bar standards.

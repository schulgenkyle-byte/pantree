# The Roaring Rooftop

**Source:** *Vanity Fair* hotel-bar columns, 1925 through 1928. Harry MacElhone, *Barflies and Cocktails*, 1927.
**Setting:** Manhattan, August 1926. The hotel rooftop that operates "for residents only" and admits anyone in evening dress.
**Guests:** 12. Three hours. 7pm to 10pm.

---

## The drinks

**French 75.** Harry MacElhone, Harry's New York Bar, Paris, 1915. Named for the French 75-millimeter field gun. The kick is the same.

One ounce gin. Half ounce lemon. Half ounce simple syrup. Three ounces champagne. Shake the gin, lemon, syrup with ice. Strain into a flute. Top with cold champagne. Long lemon peel.

**White Lady.** Harry MacElhone, 1929 reformulation at Harry's New York Bar. Earlier versions used crème de menthe. The 1929 build is the one that lived.

One and a half ounces gin. Three-quarter ounce Cointreau. Three-quarter ounce lemon. One egg white. Dry shake. Wet shake with ice. Strain into a coupe.

**Boulevardier.** Erskine Gwynne, Harry's New York Bar, 1927. Gwynne edited an expatriate Paris monthly called *Boulevardier*. The drink is a Negroni with bourbon in place of gin, and the bourbon makes it American.

One and a half ounces bourbon. One ounce Campari. One ounce sweet vermouth. Stir with ice. Strain into a coupe. Orange peel.

**Last Word.** Detroit Athletic Club, circa 1916. Forgotten for fifty years. Rediscovered in 2003 by Murray Stenson at the Zig Zag Café in Seattle, who pulled it out of Ted Saucier's 1951 *Bottoms Up*. The recipe is four equal parts, which is the kind of recipe a tired bartender writes down before service.

Three-quarter ounce gin. Three-quarter ounce green Chartreuse. Three-quarter ounce maraschino. Three-quarter ounce lime. Shake with ice. Strain into a coupe.

**Champagne Cocktail.** Pre-Prohibition holdover. Standardized by Jerry Thomas in 1862. The drink for guests who don't want to commit.

One sugar cube saturated with Angostura. Drop it into a flute. Fill with cold champagne. Lemon peel.

## The food

A rooftop in August takes a different kind of menu than a basement bar in February. Most plates here are cold and small, built to be eaten standing, and held in the palm with one hand. The hot bite is a small one. The wind is the third guest.

**Gougères.** Cheese choux puffs. Aged Gruyère, black pepper, baked the morning of. Twelve per twelve guests, plus the inevitable second pass.

**Smoked trout on pumpernickel.** Pumpernickel rounds. Horseradish cream. Smoked trout. Dill. Lemon zest.

**Watermelon and feta on toothpicks.** An American hotel-bar invention. Watermelon cubed. Feta crumbled. Mint leaf. Toothpick. Era-correct to within a decade.

**Olives and almonds.** Marcona almonds with sea salt. Castelvetrano olives. Manzanilla olives. A small bowl of each. Refill twice across the three hours.

**Beef carpaccio crostini.** Thin-sliced raw eye-of-round. Capers. Shaved Parmesan. Lemon. Olive oil. On toasted baguette. Slice the beef the morning of, plate just before guests arrive.

**Macarons.** Store-bought is fine. The era did not have macarons in America yet; they hit American shores in the 1930s. The rooftop sensibility tolerates the slight anachronism for the visual.

## Host timeline

Three hours before guests arrive:
- Bake the gougères. Cool covered.
- Toast the baguette slices for the carpaccio. Slice the trout.

Two hours before:
- Slice the carpaccio, or have the butcher do it. Plate. Cover and refrigerate.

Ninety minutes before:
- Cube the watermelon. Crumble the feta. Pluck the mint. Assemble the picks. Refrigerate.

Sixty minutes before:
- Chill the champagne, the gin, the glassware. Set the rooftop bar against the wind. Cut the lemon peels.

Thirty minutes before:
- Pour yourself a Champagne Cocktail. Walk the perimeter. Light the candles in their hurricanes against the wind.

Zero:
- Guests arrive in evening dress. The first French 75 is the photograph everyone takes.

## Music

Hot jazz with a romantic edge. Bix Beiderbecke and the Wolverines. The Original Memphis Five. Annette Hanshaw's vocals on the slow numbers. 100 to 130 BPM, fast enough to lift the room.

The in-app playlist titled "Manhattan Rooftop, 1926" loads when you open the menu.

## Shopping list

For twelve guests.

London Dry gin (1.5 × 750ml). Bourbon (1 × 750ml). Sweet vermouth (1 × 750ml). Cointreau (small). Campari (1 × 750ml). Green Chartreuse (small). Maraschino liqueur (small). Angostura bitters. Sugar cubes. Brut champagne (4 × 750ml; a credible non-vintage producer is the right move). Lemons (10). Limes (4). Oranges (3). Eggs (4). Gougère ingredients (flour, eggs, milk, butter, Gruyère, salt, black pepper). Pumpernickel bread. Prepared horseradish. Crème fraîche. Smoked trout (8oz). Fresh dill. Lemon zest. Watermelon (1 small). Feta (8oz). Fresh mint. Toothpicks. Marcona almonds (8oz). Castelvetrano olives. Manzanilla olives. Eye-of-round (8oz). Capers. Parmigiano-Reggiano. Baguette. Extra-virgin olive oil. Macarons (24, assorted).

Roughly $245 for twelve guests, champagne included. The bourbon, Campari, vermouth, and Chartreuse are the pantry investments.

## Faithful and modern-flexible

The Last Word is the drink with no easy non-alcoholic substitute. For alcohol-free guests, the temperance database offers the Cinderella, orange over lemon over pineapple over grenadine, finished with ginger ale in a flute. A 1923 American hotel-bar standard. The beef carpaccio becomes a roasted-beet carpaccio for vegetarian hosts; the visual carries. The macarons can be omitted entirely for budget reasons. The era will not notice.
